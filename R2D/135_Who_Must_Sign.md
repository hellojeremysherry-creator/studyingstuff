§ 135 Who Must Sign
Where a memorandum of a contract within the Statute is signed by fewer than all parties to the contract 
and the Statute is not otherwise satisfied, the contract is enforceable against the signers but not 
against the others.
## Comments & Illustrations
### Comment
a.  The "party to be charged."  Section 4 of the English Statute of Frauds required signature of the agreement, 
or some memorandum or note thereof, "by the party to be charged therewith, or some other person thereunto 
by him lawfully authorized." Section 17 referred to signature "by the parties to be charged by such contract or 
their agents thereunto lawfully authorized." Both forms of words are generally read to refer to the party to be 
charged in the legal proceeding, not the party or parties to be bound by the contract.  In a few states, however, 
either by statute or by decision, the memorandum of a land contract is required to be signed only by the lessor 
or vendor.  See Comment b to § 133.
b.  Agency.  A memorandum may be signed by an agent of a party with the same effect as if the party had 
signed personally.  Unless the Statute so provides, written authorization is unnecessary, but the power to sign 
cannot be orally conferred on the other party to the transaction.  The same third person may be the agent of 
both parties to sign a memorandum, and an auctioneer has irrevocable power to sign for both buyer and seller 
for a reasonable time on the day of sale.  See Restatement, Second, Agency §§ 24, 30.
## Reporter'S Notes
This Section is based on former § 211.  See 4 Williston, Contracts §§ 586-89 (3d ed. 1961); 2 Corbin, Contracts 
§§ 523-25 (1950 & Supp. 1971).
Comment a.  Statutes requiring the signature of the lessor or grantor with respect to land contracts are found in 
several states.  For decisions that the lessor or vendor is the "party to be charged," though he is the party 
seeking to enforce the contract, see Palmer v. Wheeler, 258 Or. 41, 481 P.2d 68 (1971) (applying California law 
and citing cases from several states); Klatch v. Simpson, 237 Ky. 84, 34 S.W.2d 951 (1931); Patterson v. 
Davis, 28 Tenn. App. 571, 192 S.W.2d 227 (1945); Annot., 46 A.L.R.3d 619 (1972); cf.  Di Bennedetto v. Di 
Rocco, 372 Pa. 302, 93 A.2d 474 (1953) (applying statute on creation of interest in land).  Signature by both 
parties seems to be required by Rouker v. Richardson, 49 Idaho 337, 288 P. 167 (1930); Clegg v. Brannan, 111 
Tex. 367, 234 S.W. 1076 (1921). But cf.  Union Cent. Life Ins. Co. v. Nielson, 62 Idaho 483, 114 P.2d 252 
(1941); Empire Steel Corp. v. Omni Steel Corp., 378 S.W.2d 905 (Tex. Civ. App. 1964).
Comment b.  The Statutes of Frauds of many states require written authority to validate signature by an agent 
either in all cases or for land contracts.
As to agency, see Illustration 11 to § 131.  For discussion of the authority of auctioneers and real estate brokers 
to sign memoranda as agents, see Del Rio Land, Inc. v. Haumont, 118 Ariz. 1, 574 P.2d 469 (Ct. App. 1977). 
As to a husband's authority to sign on behalf of his wife, compare Schremp v. Dubrowin, 257 Md. 623, 263 A.2d 
827 (1970), with Pettigrew v. Denwalt, 431 P.2d 333 (Okl. 1967). As to authority of a corporate officer, and the 
termination of that authority, see Computer Servicenters, Inc. v. Beacon Mfg. Co., 328 F. Supp. 653 (D.S.C. 
1970), aff'd, 443 F.2d 906 (4th Cir. 1971).
