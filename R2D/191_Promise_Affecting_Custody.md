§ 191 Promise Affecting Custody
A promise affecting the right of custody of a minor child is unenforceable on grounds of public policy 
unless the disposition as to custody is consistent with the best interest of the child.
## Comments & Illustrations
### Comment
a.  Rationale.  The custody of minor children is, like marriage, an important subject of public concern.  A 
promise by one entitled to the custody of a minor child to transfer the custody to another or not to reclaim 
custody already transferred to another is unenforceable unless it is consistent with the child's best interest.  
Such promises are typically found in separation agreements between parents, and the fact that the person to 
whom custody is transferred is a parent is an important, although not controlling, factor in showing that the 
transfer is in the interest of the child.  Even where enforcement of a promise disposing of custody is not 
precluded on grounds of public policy, the disposition is still subject to the plenary supervision of the court.  
Similar rules apply to visitation rights.
### Illustrations
1.  A and B, the parents of a child of ten, make an otherwise valid separation agreement in which A promises to 
give up custody of the child to B.  Whether or not A's promise is enforceable depends on whether custody by B 
is consistent with the best interest of the child.
2.  A and B, the parents of a child of ten, promise to give up custody of the child to C, a stranger, in return for 
C's promise to support the child.  The promises of A, B and C affect A's and B's custody rights in a minor child 
and unless the court finds that these promises are consistent with the best interest of the child, they are 
unenforceable on grounds of public policy.
## Reporter'S Notes
This Section is derived from former § 583.  The requirement of the former section that the transfer of custody be 
from one parent to another has been dropped in favor of a general requirement that the transfer be consistent 
with the best interest of the child.  See 15 Williston, Contracts § 1744A (3d ed. 1972).
Comment a.  Illustration 1 is new.  See In re Custody of Neal, 260 Pa. Super Ct. 151, 393 A.2d 1057 (1978); 
Marriage of Meier, 36 Or. App. 685, 585 P.2d 713 (1978); Cook v. Cobb, 271 S.C. 136, 245 S.E.2d 612 (1978) 
(mother vs. paternal grandparents).  Contrast Golz v. Children's Bureau, 326 So.2d 865 (La.), appeal dism'd, 
426 U.S. 901 (1976). Illustration 2 is based on Illustration 1 to former § 583.  See also Annots., 74 A.L.R.3d 421 
(1976), 78 A.L.R.3d 770 (1977). For cases upholding such contracts, see In re Shirk's Estate, 186 Kan. 311, 
350 P.2d 1 (1960); Catholic Charities of Diocese of Galveston v. Harper, 161 Tex. 21, 337 S.W.2d 111 (1960); 
cf.  Nelson v. Wilson, 97 S.W.2d 287 (Tex. Civ. App. 1936), error ref.  See also Goldstein, Freud and Solnit, 
Beyond the Best Interests of the Child (1973).  Under the "parental rights doctrine," a natural parent's rights, 
while still subject to the child's best interests, is presumptively superior to those of a third party (including 
grandparents and other relatives).  Compare Commonwealth ex rel. Strunk v. Cummins, 258 Pa. Super. Ct. 
326, 392 A.2d 817 (1978) and Hooks v. Ellerbe, 257 Pa. Super. Ct. 219, 390 A.2d 791 (1978) with Cook v. 
Cobb, supra, and Commonwealth ex rel. Murphy v. Walters, 258 Pa. Super. Ct. 418, 392 A.2d 863 (1978). 
Strunk, Cook and Murphy all involve agreements, but only Cook discusses this factor.  For a strong criticism of 
the parental rights doctrine, with valuable background material, see McGough & Shindell, Coming of Age: The 
Best Interests of the Child Standard in Parent-Third Party Custody Disputes, 27 Emory L. J. 209 (1978).
