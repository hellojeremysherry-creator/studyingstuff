## Chapter 14
Illegality, Violation of Public Policy, and Lack of Contractual Capacity

## A  Introduction
The doctrines discussed in Chapter 13 are intended to counter specific types of improper conduct in the contracting process. They share the fundamental basis of protecting one party against a manifestation of assent that was induced by improper means, whether it be dishonesty, an improper threat, abuse of power or trust, or oppressive bargaining. In allowing the victim of improper bargaining to avoid the contract or to seek one of the other remedies covered in Chapter 13, the court takes into account the competing public policies of freedom of contract and the protection of a party from dishonest or overbearing conduct. There are other situations in which a contract may be voidable, not because of the improper bargaining conduct of a party, but because the contract violates a rule of law or an important public policy or has been entered into by a party who lacks the capacity to contract. To seek avoidance of the contract on the grounds of one of these defects, the party claiming rescission does not have to prove bargaining unfairness or oppressive terms. If, in addition to the problem of illegality, violation of public policy, or incapacity, one of the parties has engaged in improper bargaining, this fact is an additional relevant consideration in the resolution of the case, and the doctrines of fraud, duress, or unconscionability could furnish additional or alternative grounds for avoidance. As intimated above, the common remedy under the doctrines in this chapter is avoidance of the contract. If the problem is lack of contractual capacity, the right of avoidance lies with the incapacitated party. In this respect, incapacity is akin to fraud, duress, undue influence, and unconscionability, in which only one of the parties (the victim or the person lacking capacity) has the right to claim avoidance. The situation is different with illegality or a violation of public policy. These doctrines are not dependent on a showing that one of the parties was incapacitated or victimized, so it could be either party who seeks avoidance of the contract. Although avoidance of the contract is based on the protection of the public

interest, it is not “the public” (in the person of a public official or a group of concerned citizens) that sues to prevent enforcement of the contract. Rather, one of the parties raises the transaction’s illegality or violation of public policy as a basis for avoiding a contract or contractual commitment. It is also possible that a court could raise illegality sua sponte. Section B deals with contracts that are illegal — the contract violates a statute or the common law. For example, a contract for the sale of a prohibited drug may be fully consensual, fairly bargained, and on fair terms. Nevertheless, if the seller breaches the contract by failing to deliver the drugs and the buyer sues for damages, the seller could successfully raise the defense that the contract is illegal. Similarly, if the seller delivered the drugs to the buyer, but the buyer did not pay for them, the buyer’s defense of illegality would likely defeat the seller’s suit for payment. It may even preclude the seller from getting restitutionary relief for the buyer’s unjust enrichment — the illegality of the transaction may deprive the seller of all relief. The sale of the prohibited drug is not merely illegal, but is also a criminal offense, so it is not hard to see why the court would refuse to enforce the contract. (In addition, one or both of the parties could be subject to criminal prosecution.) However, many illegal contracts are not crimes, but are merely a violation of the law that prohibits contracts of that kind. For example, a state statute may forbid the transfer of a liquor license and may void any such contract of transfer. Even though the parties have not committed a criminal offense in entering into the contract, the public interest in not allowing such a contract likewise most likely will result in nonenforcement. Section C deals with contracts that violate public policy. Although a contract may not be illegal, its nature or purpose may be so contrary to the public interest that a court concludes that enforcement of the contract would violate an important public policy. For example, it is not illegal for a contract to absolve a party from liability for its own negligence, but in some circumstances a court may find that such a disclaimer is so harmful to the public interest in holding a tortfeasor liable for its negligence that it violates public policy. Similarly, a noncompetition clause (a provision in an employment contract forbidding a resigning employee from working

for a competitor of the employer for a stated period in a stated area) is not generally illegal, but if the provision goes beyond the protection of the employer’s legitimate interests, it may violate important public policies that protect employees and enhance competition in the marketplace. The dividing line between illegal contracts and those that violate public policy can be quite blurry. If courts invariably refuse to uphold particular contracts on the ground that they violate public policy, the judicial precedent could ultimately become firm enough to constitute a complete bar on that type of contract, making it, in effect, illegal. Although illegality and violation of public policy could lead to avoidance of the entire contract, it is possible that the contract as a whole is not offensive, but it contains a term that is. In that case, a court may find that the most appropriate resolution is to excise the offending term and enforce the rest of the contract. The decision to sever an offending term, rather than to avoid the contract as a whole, depends on several factors, including the seriousness of the violation of law or public policy, the nature of the contract, and the appropriateness of enforcing the contract without the term. Section D deals with contracts entered into by minors. The basic rule is that a minor usually is able to avoid contractual commitments. The policy underlying the rule is that a minor needs protection from immature decision making and possible exploitation by others. However, this does not mean that the minor needs to prove immaturity or exploitation. Usually, the mere objective fact of minority is enough to permit avoidance. Section E covers contracts entered into by adults who suffer from a mental illness or defect that impairs their ability to form genuine contractual intent. Lack of contractual capacity is not an issue in most contracts, and the law presumes, in the absence of proof to the contrary, that all adults have the mental ability to enter binding contractual relationships. However, in modern law1 this presumption can be overturned by a showing that at the time of contracting, a party to the contract suffered from some mental illness or defect that made the party incapable of forming the requisite contractual intent.

## B  Illegality
A contract is illegal if it violates a statute or rule of common law. There is a broad and well-established common law doctrine, expressed in the Latin maxim ex turpi causa non oritur actio (no action arises out of an immoral act), that a court will not give relief to a plaintiff arising out of the plaintiff’s illegal conduct. In its absolute form, this doctrine denies all relief to the wrongdoing plaintiff, so that the plaintiff can neither enforce an illegal transaction nor seek any other relief, such as restitution of a benefit given to the other party in the course of the transaction. This is a general principle of law, not confined to contracts cases, but applicable elsewhere — for example, where a plaintiff is injured in the course of committing an illegal act and claims damages from the tortfeasor. In the area of contracts, the ex turpi causa principle applies where the plaintiff seeks relief arising out of an illegal contract. The absolute bar of the ex turpi causa maxim is qualified by another well-established common law principle, reflected in the maxim in pari delicto potior est conditio defendentis (where the parties are in equal guilt, the position of the defendant is the stronger). This maxim forms the basis of a rule called the in pari delicto rule. The maxim suggests that the court will not completely bar the plaintiff’s remedy but will only refuse relief where the plaintiff was as or more guilty than the defendant in entering into the illegal transaction. On its face, the language of the maxim focuses on the relative guilt of the parties. However, in its application by courts, the in pari delicto rule is much more complex. Where the parties have entered into an illegal contract, the court does not merely ask whether the plaintiff (the party seeking to enforce the contract or claiming some other remedy arising out of the defendant’s breach) is equally or more guilty than the defendant. Although this is an important consideration weighed by the court, the inquiry is much more far-ranging and involves a balancing of several factors in making the decision of whether the defendant can avoid the contract

on grounds of illegality, thereby depriving the plaintiff of a remedy arising out of the defendant’s breach. The in pari delicto rule is treated as an equitable defense, so when the defendant raises it in response to the plaintiff’s breach of contract claim, the court exercises considerable discretion in setting the balance. The factors that the court weighs, in addition to relative guilt, are the seriousness of the illegality, the purpose of the law that has been violated, the public interest in refusing or allowing a remedy, and the equities between the parties. The public interest is the most dominant consideration, and it encompasses many concerns: the reluctance of the court to aid a lawbreaker; the public policy of upholding the law that has been violated; the creation of a disincentive to future violations of the law; whether refusal of a remedy would further or hinder the purpose of the law that has been violated; and the impact of refusing or granting relief on the public good. In some cases, the court may also take into account the remedy sought — whether it is enforcement of the contract or restitution of benefits conferred on the defendant under the contract. A court may be more inclined to grant restitutionary relief because that does not involve the enforcement of the illegal contract. An example illustrates the nature and scope of the court’s balancing process: An employer enters into a contract to employ an undocumented immigrant who does not have a permit to work in the United States. The employee works for the employer for a month and then quits. The employer refuses to pay the employee the wages earned during that month and the employee sues to recover the wages. Both parties violated the law in entering into the contract — the employer was forbidden from hiring the worker and the worker was forbidden from working without a work permit. If both parties knew that they were acting illegally, it could be that they were in pari delicto. So, if relative guilt was the simple scope of the inquiry, the employee would be barred from claiming the wages due under the contract. However, there is much more to the balancing process, which may lead the court to conclude that the employer should not be able to raise the in pari delicto defense to resist paying the employee. The court may be influenced by several reasons. First, an undocumented worker’s violation of the law forbidding the person to

work is not a heinous offense, but more the infringement of a regulatory statute. Second, because the employment contract has already been performed and the illegality is not ongoing, there is no public benefit in depriving the employee of earned wages. Third, holding the employer accountable for paying the wages removes the benefit of getting free labor, thereby aiming the disincentive at employers, who may be more likely to respond to it because they are more sophisticated, less needy, and in a stronger bargaining position. Fourth, the equities favor allowing relief to the employee because otherwise the employer would be unjustly enriched by the free labor. If, despite these considerations, the court is still reluctant to enforce the contract, giving the employee restitutionary relief based on quantum meruit allows the court to permit a remedy based on unjust enrichment, rather than on enforcement of the illegal contract. ## C  Contracts In Violation Of Public
## Policy
Courts do not always draw a clear distinction between contracts that are illegal and contracts that violate public policy, but a clear distinction between them can be identified for purposes of analysis. An illegal contract offends a statutory or common law rule. A contract that violates public policy does not violate the law, but is so contrary to the public interest that the court concludes that it should not be enforced. Courts do not always articulate this distinction, and it is quite common to find opinions (such as Danzig) that refer an illegal contract as one that offends “law or public policy.” When courts use this language, they often mean an illegal contract. Also, the distinction between an illegal contract and one that offends public policy is a matter of degree, because if there is consistent judicial precedent for refusing to enforce a contract on grounds of public policy, at some point that precedent becomes so well established that it would be more correct to think of the contract having become illegal at common law. Nevertheless, it helps understanding to make the distinction clear and to recognize that the process of analysis differs between these two categories. A significant difference is that where the contract is not prohibited by law, it is not necessary to evaluate the parties’ relative guilt under the in pari delicto rule. However, some of the other factors relevant to the determination of whether a contract should be avoided as illegal — the balance of the equities, and the public interest — also play a role in public policy analysis. In some cases, the contract as a whole is unenforceable because it offends public policy. In others, it may only be term or portion of the contract that should not be enforced on public policy grounds. Where that happens, the court may leave the contract in force, but just excise the term that violates public policy, provided the violation of public policy does not permeate the contract and severance does not significantly remake the contract. (These are the same

considerations that a court takes into account in deciding whether to eliminate an unconscionable term in a contract.) The decision to refuse to enforce a contract or contract term on policy grounds is more tenuous than a refusal to enforce an illegal contract, because there is no statute or rule of common law that invalidates the contract. If the contract was entered into voluntarily by both parties who desired the contract and neither was subjected to unfair bargaining, the public policy of freedom of contract pulls strongly in the direction of enforcement. Therefore, it is only when the public interest in not enforcing the contract is strong enough to outweigh upholding freedom of contract that a court will consider it appropriate to refuse enforcement by allowing avoidance of the contract as a whole or of an offending term. Where there has been some bargaining unfairness or adhesion in the formation of the contract, the weight of the policy of freedom of contract is lessened, so that it may be easier to persuade the court to allow avoidance on policy grounds. 3. Whether the Waivers Were Fairly Entered Into
The third Jones factor requires us to examine whether the contract was fairly entered into. A contract is fairly entered into if one party is not so obviously disadvantaged with respect to bargaining power that the resulting contract essentially places him at the mercy of the other party’s negligence. When engaging in this analysis, we examine the nature of the service involved, the circumstances surrounding the formation of the contract, and whether the services provided are available from a source other than the party with which the plaintiff contracted. The Colorado Court of Appeals has identified

“[p]ossible examples of unfair disparity in bargaining power [as] includ[ing] agreements between employers and employees and between common carriers or public utilities and members of the public.” Stone [v. Life Time Fitness, Inc., 411 P.3d 225] at 229 [(Colo. App. 2016)]. It has also expressly acknowledged an unfair disparity in bargaining power in residential landlord-tenant relationships, presumably based in part on its holding “that housing rental is a matter of practical necessity to the public.” Stanley v. Creighton Co., 911 P.2d 705, 708 (Colo. App. 1996). But the Colorado Court of Appeals has also held that “this type of unfair disparity is generally not implicated when a person contracts with a business providing recreational services.” Stone, 411 P.3d at 229. This is because recreational activities are not essential services or practically necessary, and therefore a person is not at the mercy of a business’s negligence when entering an exculpatory agreement involving recreational activities. As we have previously explained, “Colorado courts have repeatedly emphasized that... because recreational businesses do not provide ‘essential’ services of ‘practical necessity[,]’ individuals are generally free to walk away if they do not wish to assume the risks described” in an exculpatory agreement. One type of contractual provision that is commonly evaluated to assess its validity on grounds of public policy is a noncompetition provision. This is a provision in, say, an employment or partnership contract in which the employee or partner agrees not to engage in commercial activity in competition with the employer or partnership for a specified time, in a specified geographic area, following termination of the employment or partnership. Noncompetition provisions are also commonly found in contracts for the sale of a business, in terms of which the seller agrees not to open a competing business for a stated time and in a stated area after the sale. The policy of freedom of contract favors upholding a validly agreed noncompetition clause. However, this must be balanced against the policy of sustaining an open and competitive market. With regard to some types of contract, the policy considerations against enforcing noncompetition provisions are so strong that the law invalidates them completely. In those situations, the noncompetition agreement is ineffective as a matter of law and is illegal, rather than a violation of public policy. For example, as noted below, in some states, the policy of protecting employees from restrictions on earning a livelihood has motivated legislation that invalidates noncompetition clauses in employment contracts. In some cases, the nature of the contract motivates the decision to make a noncompetition provision illegal. For example, the policy of

allowing clients free access to the lawyer of their choice is reflected in ABA Model Rule of Professional Conduct 5.6(a), which forbids a provision in lawyers’ employment or partnership agreements that restricts the right of a departing lawyer to practice after termination of the relationship. In all other situations, courts evaluate the validity of a noncompetition clause by following an approach known as the “rule of reason.” A court applying the rule of reason will uphold a noncompetition clause to the extent that it is reasonable as to its duration, the geographic area that it covers, and the scope and extent of the activity that it restrains. In deciding this question, the court examines the totality of the circumstances, including the degree to which the provision is needed to protect the legitimate interests of the party in whose favor it operates, any undue hardship that it will impose on the restrained party, and the general public interest. (If an unfairly rigorous noncompetition provision is included in a contract of adhesion, the party subject to the noncompetition provision could attack it on the alternative ground that it is unconscionable.) When a court evaluates a noncompetition clause under the rule of reason, it takes into account the nature of the relationship formed by the contract and covered by the clause. When the noncompetition provision is part of a contract for the sale of a business, a court is likely to give it the greatest degree of deference. Here, the clause is usually imposed to protect the goodwill of the business that the buyer has just bought and paid for. It is not difficult to see that the buyer of a business could lose a substantial basis of the bargain if, immediately after the sale, the seller opened a competing business in the same market. Noncompetition clauses in partnership agreements are designed to protect the partnership business from competition from a partner who leaves the partnership to open or join a competing business. As a former owner of the business, the dissociating partner may be in a position to take away customers, clients, staff, or partnership secrets, so a reasonable restraint on the disassociating partner’s ability to compete with the partnership could be justifiable. Noncompetition clauses in employment contracts are completely barred in some states,2 and in others are usually subject

to careful scrutiny. The principal reason for restricting or forbidding noncompetition provisions in employment contracts is that an employee may have little to take away from the business apart from the employee’s own skill. If so, a restraint on the employee’s ability to remain in the field after leaving the employment does not really protect a legitimate property interest of the employer and may make it difficult for the employee to earn a living. (A noncompetition clause must be distinguished from a more justifiable provision in a contract that forbids the employee to take property that belongs to the employer, such as customer lists and trade secrets.) The next case shows how a court weighs the countervailing interests of the employer, the employee, and society in deciding on the validity of a noncompetition provision in an employment contract. ## D  Incapacity Based On Minority
1. General Principles
The basic general rule relating to a minor’s contract is that a minor does not have the capacity to enter into a binding contract, so the contract is avoidable at the instance of the minor. The contract is avoidable, not void, which means that the minor may elect not to seek avoidance of the contract, leaving it in force. The other party cannot use the minor’s lack of capacity to rescind the contract. The minor’s right to avoid the contract continues throughout minority and may extend for a reasonable time after the minor attains majority. The public policy basis of minority doctrine is the protection of minors who are deemed to be unable to act with maturity and rationality in their own interests in entering the contract, making them vulnerable to exploitation. However, although exploitation is a concern, it is not an element that must be shown for avoidance — the policy of protecting the minor usually outweighs concern about protecting the other party’s reliance interest, even if that party did not act unfairly or exploitatively. Therefore, with a few narrow exceptions, a minor can avoid the contract on the simple basis of minority without having to show that the other party was guilty of improper bargaining or deliberate advantage-taking. The age at which a person attains contractual capacity is typically fixed by statute. In most states it is 18, but the legislature might choose a different age. Until that age, the person is a minor. (Sometimes the old-fashioned term “infant” is used, which sounds odd in light of modern usage, especially where the “infant” seeking avoidance is an adolescent close to the age of majority.) It is arbitrary to fix the transition from incompetence to capability at a defined birthday, but this objective, easily ascertainable test is simpler and more certain than a case-by-case evaluation of the subjective attributes of each individual minor. The disadvantage of this test is that it does not distinguish between young children and

teenagers who may in fact be old enough to make rational economic decisions. In addition, with the rapid rise of electronic commerce, minors surely constitute a significant segment of the market for online goods and services, and many of these minors may have facility and experience with online purchasing. The greater participation of minors in the marketplace, and a changed perception of the contractual competence of adolescents may, over time, cause a shift in minority doctrine. To some extent, this has already begun to occur as legislatures carve out transaction-specific exceptions to the common law rule. Nevertheless, for the present, the law uses a bright-line objective test to determine the contractual capacity of minors.
2. Disaffirmance and Ratification
As noted above, in most cases a minor’s lack of contractual capacity makes the contract voidable, not void, so that the minor may choose to avoid (or disaffirm) the contract on grounds of incapacity or to keep it in force. A minor may disaffirm the contract, expressly or by conduct, at any time before reaching majority. Once the contract is disaffirmed, it comes to an end, and the minor cannot revive and seek to enforce it. If the minor has not disaffirmed the contract by the time of reaching majority, the ex-minor may be deemed to have ratified it, thereby losing the right to avoid it. Ratification need not be express; it could be by conduct if the ex-minor continues to retain or receive a benefit under the contract after becoming a major, or simply if the ex- minor takes no action to disaffirm the contract within a reasonable time after majority. If the ex-minor has not expressly ratified the contract or continued to keep or accept benefits under the contract upon becoming a major, but has simply taken no action at all, the power of disaffirmance does not end immediately upon reaching the age of majority. The ex-minor is accorded a reasonable time after majority to disaffirm. The question of what period of time is reasonable is factual and is determined under all the circumstances of the case. Courts commonly allow the ex-minor some leeway in delaying disaffirmance after reaching majority. Given that the change

from minority to majority is purely arbitrary, based on age and not on a true newfound maturity, the test of reasonable time should take some account of the amount of time that it would take for the ex- minor to become mature enough to make a rational decision on whether to keep the contract in force. Other considerations that may influence a court to find ratification by failure to disaffirm in time are the degree of reliance, if any, on the part of the major party, the degree and extent of prejudice (especially ongoing prejudice) that would be suffered by the major, and the ex-minor’s diligence or lack of it in seeking disaffirmance. California Family Code section 6700 provides that... “a minor may make a contract in the same manner as an adult, subject to the power of disaffirmance” provided by section 6710. California courts recognize that “sound policy considerations support this provision.” Berg v. Traylor, 148 Cal. App. 4th 809, 818, 56 Cal. Rptr. 3d 140 (2007). “‘The law shields minors from their lack of judgment and experience and under certain conditions vests in them the right to disaffirm their contracts. Although in many instances such disaffirmance may be a hardship upon those who deal with an infant, the right to avoid his contracts is conferred by law upon a minor for his protection against his own improvidence and the designs of others. It is the policy of the law to protect a minor against himself and his indiscretions and immaturity as well as against the machinations of other people and to discourage adults from contracting with an infant. of a minor’s contract might have been avoided by declining to enter into the contract.’” Id. (quoting Niemann v. Deverich, 98 Cal. App. 2d 787, 793, 221 P.2d 178 (1950)). Express notice to the other party is unnecessary. Disaffirmation by a minor rescinds the entire contract, rendering it a nullity. An action for disaffirmation is one in equity, governed in many respects by the rules relating to rescission of contracts, and the trial court is vested with a broad discretion to see that equity is done. 4. Contracts Entered into by a Minor with Parental
Assistance or Executed by a Parent on Behalf of a Minor There is no general and universally applicable rule on whether a minor is bound by a contract entered into by the minor with the consent and assistance of a parent,4 or executed by a parent on behalf of the minor. The binding force of such contracts depends on the nature of the contract and the law of the particular jurisdiction. With regard to most types of contract, a minor is bound by and cannot disaffirm the obligations assumed by the minor with parental assistance or assumed on the minor’s behalf by a parent. This principle is based on the right of parents to make decisions for their minor children and on the practical necessity of having some way to create legal obligations that are binding on a minor. However, with regard to some contracts, the policy of protecting minors takes precedence over these considerations and permits the minor to disaffirm, notwithstanding the role of a parent in the execution of the contract. A waiver of liability for injury (an exculpatory agreement), such as that entered into by the adult party in Brigance in Section C, is one type of contract that raises these countervailing considerations. It is common for a provider of services to a minor to require a parent to execute a waiver of liability on behalf of a minor child to enable the

child to participate in an activity that could result in injury to the child. The waiver of liability may be crucial to the service provider, which may not have the financial resources to offer the service without insulating itself from the risk of liability. However, if the minor is injured, the waiver could impose severe hardship on the minor, who has lost the right to hold the service provider accountable for the injury. Some courts have upheld these waivers, provided that they are not avoidable under some general principle of contract law (such as duress or unconscionability). Others have refused to uphold them, even though they may have been binding on an adult party. The next case falls into the latter category. Although its focus is on whether a parent can validly enter into a release of liability on behalf of a minor, its public policy analysis would also make it a good candidate for inclusion in Section C. It provides insight into the courts’ role in formulating general public policy in the context of resolving a particular contract dispute between parties to litigation. The question in the case is whether the court should reverse a judicial precedent that had been in force for over a century, that a minor cannot be bound by a liability waiver entered into on the minor’s behalf by a parent. The public policy analysis centers on the question of whether the court should overturn this well-established precedent. Because the rule was judicially created, the court has the authority to change it if it concludes that the public policy considerations have changed since the time that the rule was first established. All members of the court agree on the result of the case — that a parent cannot bind a minor to a waiver of liability for personal injury. However, the justices disagree on the rationale for this decision, and each delivers a separate concurring opinion. There is therefore no majority opinion of the court. We include here the opinion of Justice Young (referred to as the “lead opinion”), which addresses the concerns that courts must weigh when asked by a party to litigation to resolve a case on the basis of public policy that has not been incorporated into legislation. We then include a summary of one of the concurring opinions, which offers a different perspective.

5. Exceptions to a Minor’s Lack of Capacity, Especially
Contracts for Necessaries

There are a number of specific exceptions to the minor’s right to avoid a contract at will. Many of these exceptions have been created by statutes enabling minors to enter particular types of binding contracts. Apart from statutory exceptions, the common law recognizes a limited number of circumstances under which a minor is bound by a contract or is responsible to pay the other party the reasonable value of what the minor received under the contract. The most important common law exception relates to a contract for necessaries. The standard definition of “necessaries” covers goods or services essential for the minor’s health and sustenance or reasonably necessary for the preservation or enjoyment of life, such as food, medical needs, clothes, or shelter. “Necessaries” is a broader term than “necessities,” and it can go beyond goods or services essential to sustain life to include items that are not luxuries but are useful and appropriate, given the minor’s reasonable standard of living. Because the definition is open ended and fact based, different courts may reach different conclusions over whether a particular item is a necessary, or the circumstances of one case may justify treating something as a necessary that would not be seen as such under different circumstances. For example, in Webster Street Partnership, Ltd. v. Sheridan, 220 Neb. 9, 368 N.W.2d 439 (1985), the court held that minors who had entered into an apartment lease had not contracted for a necessary. Although one would normally think of shelter as quite a basic necessity of life (and courts include it along with food and clothing in the standard definition of necessaries), the court found that it was not a necessary under the circumstances of the case because the minors could have lived with their parents. Courts express different views as to the effect of a contract for necessaries. Some treat the contract as fully enforceable against the minor. Others hold that where the contract is for necessaries, this still does not make the contract enforceable on its terms. The minor may still disaffirm the contract. However, the adult party has a claim against the minor in quasi-contract for the reasonable value of the goods or services. The issue of whether goods or services are necessaries is tied to the question of whether a minor is emancipated. A minor becomes

emancipated when the minor’s parents no longer have a duty to support the minor. This may occur where the minor lives independently of the parents. However, some courts define emancipation more narrowly, and confine it to specific situations such as marriage or enlisting in the military. If a minor is not emancipated and is entitled to parental support, it is difficult for the other party to claim that goods or services furnished to the minor were necessaries. However, this does not mean that emancipation in itself creates contractual capacity in the minor for all purposes. Rather, it just makes it more likely that a contract entered into by a minor for appropriate goods or services will qualify as a contract for necessaries. 6. Restitution on Disaffirmance
Restitution usually accompanies rescission — when a contract is rescinded, each party must restore to the other any benefit (or its value) that was received under the contract before it was avoided. This principle is discussed in Chapter 13, in which restitution followed avoidance on grounds of fraud, duress, undue influence or unconscionability on the basis that a party would be unjustly enriched by retaining a benefit under the avoided contract. This rule may not apply to its full extent where a minor disaffirms a contract. It is well established that the major party is required to restore to the minor any benefit that the major party received from the contract. It is also clear that the minor is obliged to restore to the major party any property that the minor received under the contract and still possesses at the time of disaffirmance. For example, say that the minor buys a motorbike from a major for $3,000 and pays cash on delivery of the bike. The court finds the bike not to be a necessary and permits the minor to disaffirm the contract. The minor is entitled to restitution of the $3,000 paid to the seller, and the seller is entitled to return of the bike. However, matters become more complicated if the minor no longer has the bike (say, because it was destroyed in an accident), or its value is greatly diminished (say, because it was damaged in an

accident or has depreciated as a result of use and the passage of time). In such a situation, the traditional approach is to confine the minor’s restitutionary obligation to the return of whatever contractual benefit the minor still retains at the time of disaffirmance. If the bike has been destroyed, the minor would owe nothing. If the minor still has the bike, it must be returned in its damaged or depreciated state, but the minor is not required to compensate the major party for any loss in value. The rationale for confining the minor’s liability to the return of what the minor still possesses is the policy of protecting a minor from improvident contracts and of discouraging adults from contracting with minors. Under the traditional approach, the minor would also not be liable to pay the major party for services that the major has rendered that are, by their nature, intangible and incapable of being returned. For example, in Webster Street Partnership, cited in Section D.5 above, the court held that upon disaffirmance of the lease, the major party had to repay to the minors the entire amount of rent and the security deposit received under the lease. However, the minors had no obligation to pay anything to the lessor for the value of occupying the apartment up to the time of disaffirmance. The traditional approach places the strongest emphasis on protecting the minor and allocates all the risk of contracting with a minor to the adult party. It does not hold the minor accountable for the conduct of entering the contract or dissipating the value of what was received under it. This may be appropriate where the adult has taken advantage of the minor, but it is harder to justify where the contract was on fair terms and the minor received an economic benefit, such as the right to occupy an apartment, as in Webster Street Partnership, or the right to use the bike, as in the example. Some courts recognize this and are more inclined to hold the minor responsible for compensating the major party for the value of the benefit received. However, there is no clear and consistent trend away from the traditional rule. Under some circumstances, the major party may be able to recover damages in tort because a minor below the age of contractual capacity may be old enough to be liable in tort. Therefore, a minor who commits fraud or who damages property

negligently or willfully could incur tort liability, even if immune from restitutionary liability.

## E  Incapacity Based On Mental
## Illness Or Defect
In most cases the presumption that a minor lacks contractual capacity is conclusive — the other party is not given the opportunity to rebut it by evidence that the minor was competent to enter a contract. The contrary presumption applies to adults who are rebuttably presumed to have contractual capacity. A mentally incompetent party may rebut the presumption by evidence that establishes a lack of contractual capacity at the time of entering the transaction. The burden of proving lack of capacity is on the person alleging it. The crucial time for measuring incapacity is the time of contracting. If the mental disorder is long-lasting or permanent, evidence of mental illness prior to or subsequent to that time may be relevant. However, the impairment must be shown to have existed at the time of contracting. As in the case of a minor, the purpose of incapacity doctrine is the protection of a person who lacks the ability to act rationally in the person’s own interests. Therefore, most courts treat incapacity as rendering the contract voidable by the incompetent party, not void, so if the incompetent party chooses not to avoid the contract, it is binding, and the incapacity cannot be raised to challenge the contract by the other party. A contract that is avoidable on grounds of mental incapacity can become fully enforceable by the other party if the mentally incompetent person ratifies the contract after recovering capacity. Sometimes the claim of avoidance is made, not by the incapacitated party, but by the party’s personal representative, such as a court-appointed guardian or by the party’s estate. The protection of a mentally incompetent person from exploitation is a principal goal of incapacity doctrine. As in the case of minors, a mentally incompetent person does not necessarily need to show that the contract is unfair or exploitive. However, the policy of protecting a mentally incompetent person is not as absolute as that applicable to minors, so that the need to protect the mentally incompetent party is

more carefully weighed against the need to protect the other party’s legitimate reliance. Therefore, the absence or presence of improper conduct or reason to know of the incompetence by the other party is often weighed in the balance when a court decides whether to allow avoidance of the contract. That is, in deciding whether a contract should be avoided on grounds of mental incapacity, courts usually take into account not only the degree and seriousness of the mental incompetence but also whether the vulnerability of the incompetent party attracted exploitation by the other. The standard for avoidability on grounds of mental incompetence is not uniform among the states. The strictest standard, sometimes called the “cognitive test” requires a showing that, at the time of contracting, the party had such severe mental incapacity as to be unable to understand the nature and consequences of the transaction. Many courts recognize a less rigorous standard, sometimes called the “motivational test,” which allows avoidance even if the party may have understood the transaction if the mental illness affected the party’s ability to act rationally in relation to it. Restatement, Second §15(1) recognizes both tests, stating that a person may avoid a contract if, by reason of mental illness, the person is “unable to understand in a reasonable manner the nature and consequences of the transaction” (the cognitive test) or is “unable to act in a reasonable manner in relation to the transaction” (the motivational test). The reasonable expectations of the other party play a role in the court’s decision to allow avoidance of the contract on grounds of mental incompetence. Restatement, Second §15 recognizes the relevance of the other party’s reliance interest by restricting avoidance under the motivational test to situations in which the other party has reason to know of the incompetent party’s condition and by generally recognizing the court’s discretion to refuse avoidance if the contract is on fair terms and the circumstances make avoidance unjust to the other party. Mental incompetence may take many forms deriving from illness, injury, or substance abuse. It is important to distinguish incompetence generated by these mental impairments from incompetence in business or personal affairs. Mental incompetence

cannot be used as a basis for avoidance merely because a person lacks competence in managing the transaction or has acted foolishly, recklessly, or with bad judgment. The conduct must be attributable to a psychiatrically recognized condition. This means that expert testimony in the form of psychiatric diagnosis is usually necessary to establish the basis for avoidance.
